## 1. The title

### 1.1 The title as printed

> **SHAP or LIME? Agreement, Stability, Faithfulness and Speed Across Six Models and Three
> Clinical Datasets**

Fifteen words in three parts. The first three words ask the question a practitioner actually
has; the next four name the things we measured to answer it; the last six say how wide the
evidence behind the answer is.

### 1.2 What each part of it means

| Part of the title | What it refers to in the paper |
|---|---|
| **SHAP or LIME?** | the question the paper exists to answer. "or", not "versus": we are not staging a fight between two tools, we are asking which one a person should reach for. If a reader remembers only three words of our work, these are the three. |
| **Agreement** | do the two tools rank the features the same way? RQ1 — Spearman ρ, Kendall τ, Jaccard@k, RBO. Section IV-A. |
| **Stability** | ask the same question twice and get the same answer? RQ2, and we split it three ways: re-running the tool, nudging the input, retraining the model. Section IV-B. |
| **Faithfulness** | does the explanation describe what the model actually uses? RQ3 — delete the features it ranks highest and watch the prediction fall. Section IV-C. |
| **Speed** | what does one explanation cost in seconds? RQ4, reported in the same subsection as faithfulness because the two together are the practical trade-off. Section IV-C. |
| **Across Six Models** | six classifier families, not one. This is the axis almost every earlier comparison holds fixed. |
| **and Three Clinical Datasets** | three public, de-identified medical tables — one row per patient. No medical claim is made anywhere in the paper. |

Two things are deliberately *not* in the title: our own hybrid method, and the words
*benchmark*, *reproducible* and *multi-model*. §1.3 explains the first. The second is simply
plain language — and note that **tabular** is gone too, so if anyone asks whether the scope
is clear, the abstract's first sentence says "from a table of data" and *tabular data* is
still in the keyword list.

### 1.3 Why we chose this title

The title changed three times before it settled, and the path matters, because the obvious
viva question is *"why isn't your own method in the title?"*

**Version 1 — the comparison.** SHAP versus LIME, benchmarked. Direct, but it read like a
survey, and it hid the one part of the paper that is genuinely ours.

**Version 2 — the method in front.** *"A Hybrid SHAP–LIME Consensus Method and Its Limits."*
Our supervisor asked us to lead with the contribution, so the hybrid moved to the front, and
"and Its Limits" went in at the same time: a method named in a title that then fails looks
evasive unless the title admits it up front.

**Version 3 — plainer second half.** *"A Reproducible Multi-Model Benchmark on Clinical
Tabular Data"* became *"A Repeatable Comparison Across Six Models and Three Clinical
Datasets."* Same meaning, ordinary words.

| Was | Became | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Reproducible | Repeatable | same idea, everyday word |
| Multi-Model | Across Six Models | says the actual number instead of a prefix |
| Benchmark | Comparison | "benchmark" is a term of art; "comparison" is not |
| on Clinical Tabular Data | and Three Clinical Datasets | drops "tabular", which the first line of the abstract explains anyway |

**Version 4 — back to the comparison (current).** We showed version 3 to our supervisor
and his verdict was that the title no longer represented the thesis. He was right, for a
reason worth being able to state plainly: **the hybrid is one of six research questions,
it is the only one that is a proposal of ours rather than a measurement, and it did not
clear its bar.** Leading with it put our weakest result in the shop window and left the
five questions that carry the comparison out of sight. Version 4 puts the question back in
front — *SHAP or LIME?* — and then lists the four things we measured in order to answer
it, which is what the study actually is.

| Versions 2–3 led with | Version 4 leads with |
|---|---|
| a proposal of ours that won in 0 of 18 cells | a question every practitioner has |
| "Hybrid … Consensus … and Its Limits" — three abstract nouns before any evidence | four concrete measurements: agreement, stability, faithfulness, speed |
| our contribution | our evidence |

**Is dropping "and Its Limits" less honest?** No, and this is the answer to give if it comes
up. The negative result did not move, soften or shrink. It is contribution 3 in the
introduction, it is RQ6 in the pre-registered hypothesis table (Table I), it has its own
results subsection (IV-E), it is discussed as a mechanism rather than a mishap in Section V,
and the abstract states it in one plain sentence: *"Merging the two, our own rule included,
never beat the better single tool."* What changed is the shop window, not the stock. A title
should say what a paper is about; this paper is about comparing two explainers, and the
hybrid is one of the tests we ran while doing it.

### 1.4 The four measurement words, defined once

These are the four nouns in the title. If each of us can define them in a sentence without
notes, the title defends itself.

- **Agreement** — do the two tools put the same features at the top, in the same order? We
  compare *rankings*, not scores: SHAP and LIME produce numbers on different scales, so only
  the order is comparable between them.
- **Stability** — ask the same question twice and get the same answer. Three different things
  hide inside that one word, and the paper separates them: randomness inside the tool, a tiny
  change in the patient's numbers, and retraining the model itself. An explanation can be
  bit-for-bit identical on a re-run and still be fragile.
- **Faithfulness** — the explanation describes the *model*, not the world, and not the
  explainer's own guess. Test: delete the features the explanation ranks highest and see how
  fast the prediction collapses. If it barely moves, the explanation was pointing at the wrong
  features.
- **Speed** — seconds per explanation, both tools timed on the same machine. It earns a place
  in the title because it is the one axis where the gap is not close: about 25 to 1.

Two words that used to be *in* the title still matter *inside* the paper, so keep them ready:

- **Hybrid** — not a new algorithm built from scratch. A rule that takes SHAP's output and
  LIME's output and produces one combined ranking. Nothing inside either tool is modified.
  Section III-C.
- **Consensus** — agreement, imposed rather than observed: the rule builds a single "agreed"
  ranking out of two lists that differ on about one feature in five.

## 3. The abstract

### 3.1 What an abstract has to do

Most people who ever see this paper will read the abstract and nothing else. It therefore
has to carry the whole study: the problem, what we built, how we tested it, what we found,
and whether the result can be trusted. Our supervisor's instruction was that a new reader
should get through it without difficulty, so it is written in short sentences and ordinary
words: 20 sentences averaging about 21 words each, longest 30. The version he first read had
8 sentences averaging 36 words, one of which ran to 75.

The abstract's **order** was changed once more when the title became a comparison title
(§1.3). It used to announce the hybrid immediately after the gap; now it states what the
comparison measures, describes the study, and only then introduces the hybrid. Nothing was
added or removed except the one sentence that names the four measurements, and the negative
result is still stated in the same words. The point is that a reader who has just read
*SHAP or LIME? Agreement, Stability, Faithfulness and Speed* should meet those four things
in the first few lines of the abstract, not a method they have not been told they need.

| The abstract says | What it is doing | Where the paper backs it up |
|---|---|---|

### 3.3 Why there is almost no number in the abstract

Our supervisor asked for the numbers to come out. An abstract full of decimals is hard to
read and impossible to remember, and the numbers are all in Section IV anyway. Exactly one
survives — "about 24.8 times faster" — because a speed factor is easy to grasp and it is
the single most concrete reason to prefer SHAP in practice. Even that one is not typed by
hand: it is the macro `\SpeedFactor`, generated from the measured timings.

## 4. Index Terms — the keywords

IEEE papers carry a short keyword list under the abstract. It is not decoration: it is how
the paper is indexed and found. Ours are:

> explainable artificial intelligence, SHAP, LIME, ensemble learning, model
> interpretability, tabular data, reproducibility, pre-registration

| Keyword | What it means | Why it is in our list |
|---|---|---|
| **explainable artificial intelligence** | the research field concerned with making model decisions understandable to people. Usually written XAI. | the field the paper belongs to; the term anyone searching for this work would use |
| **SHAP** | SHapley Additive exPlanations. Splits a prediction among the features using a fairness rule borrowed from cooperative game theory. | one of the two objects under study |
| **LIME** | Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations. Builds a simple model that imitates the big model near one prediction, and reports its coefficients. | the other object under study |
| **ensemble learning** | models built from many smaller models — random forests, boosted trees, stacking. | six of our classifiers are or include ensembles, and the whole point of the paper is models you cannot read directly |
| **model interpretability** | how far a human can follow why a model produced an output. | the property we are measuring around |
| **tabular data** | ordinary rows-and-columns data: one row per patient, one column per measurement. Not images, not text. | our scope. It carries the word that left the title. |
| **reproducibility** | someone else can re-run the study and get our numbers. | one of the paper's two methodological selling points |
| **pre-registration** | writing down the hypotheses and the pass/fail rules *before* looking at any result. | the other one, and the reason our negative result is credible rather than an excuse |

If asked why we did not include "clinical" or "healthcare": the datasets are benchmarks for
a computer-science question, and we make no medical claim. Indexing the paper under a
clinical term would invite the wrong readers.

## 5. Section I — Introduction

Four paragraphs, each doing one job.

**Paragraph 1 — why anyone should care.** Tree ensembles are what people actually deploy on
tabular data, and you cannot read their reasoning off their parameters. Then the legal
pressure: the EU AI Act [1] requires providers of high-risk systems to make operation
interpretable. Then the two tools, SHAP [2], [3] and LIME [4], and the observation that
their foundations differ and their failure modes are unrelated [5]. The paragraph ends on
the concrete problem: *a practitioner who runs both and gets two different top-five lists
has no principled basis for choosing.* That sentence is the paper's reason to exist.

**Paragraph 2 — the gap.** Earlier comparisons hold too much fixed: one model family or one
dataset, a fidelity metric defined locally in that paper, no hypothesis test, no
random-ranking baseline, and no statement of which output space the attributions live in.
The closing line is the one to quote in the viva: *"The gap is not a missing algorithm but a
missing controlled comparison — and that is something an experiment can close."*

**Paragraph 3 — the three contributions.** An explicit numbered list:

1. **A reproducible multi-axis benchmark** — two explainers, three fusion rules and a random
   control, over six classifiers and three datasets, 18 cells and 1,746 explained
   instances, with eleven pre-registered hypotheses, non-parametric tests, Holm correction
   inside each family, effect sizes and bootstrap intervals.
2. **A three-way decomposition of explanation stability** — explainer randomness, input
   perturbation, and variance from retraining the model. One stability number hides all
   three, and the distinction is what produces our best finding.
3. **A hybrid consensus method, and an honest account of its limits** — fidelity-weighted
   rank fusion, its leakage-safe calibration, and the strict bar it is tested against. The
   paragraph says plainly that aggregating explanations is prior work [7] and that we claim
   no priority for the idea; what is new is weighting by *measured* fidelity, fitting that
   weight on data the test never sees, and testing against the better of the two rather
   than their mean.

**Paragraph 4 — the roadmap.** One sentence pointing at the method section and the four
traps, one at the results.

A question we should expect: *why is the contributions list still there when the advisor
asked to remove a contribution section?* Because he meant the **Author Contributions**
paragraph at the back of the paper — the "who did what" statement — not the list of what the
paper contributes. Those are two different things with confusingly similar names.

## 6. Section II — Related Work

Four short paragraphs. Their job is to show we know the field and to locate the gap
precisely, not to list everything ever published.

**Paragraph 1 — what the two tools actually are.** SHAP gives each feature the Shapley
value of its marginal contribution, with TreeSHAP making that computable on tree ensembles
[2], [3]. LIME perturbs the instance, weights the perturbations by how close they are, and
fits a sparse linear model whose coefficients are the explanation [4]. Then the sentence
that matters: *the difference is not cosmetic — one attributes the model's output, the other
approximates its local function.* This is why we should not expect them to agree perfectly,
and why the disagreement is interesting rather than a bug.

**Paragraph 2 — how anyone evaluates an explanation at all.** There is no ground-truth
attribution: nobody can tell you the "correct" importance of a feature. So evaluation is
indirect. Deletion tests remove features in the order the explanation gives and watch the
prediction fall, summarised as area over the perturbation curve [8]; ERASER formalised
comprehensiveness and sufficiency [9]; Hooker et al. showed that these tests are confounded
because deleting features creates inputs the data never produced [10]; robustness is
normally estimated as a local Lipschitz constant [11]; rank-biased overlap compares two
ranked lists without a cut-off [12]. The paragraph then says both methods have weaker
guarantees in practice than in theory and both can be deliberately fooled [5], and ends:
*we take these as reasons for careful measurement rather than settled verdicts.*

**Paragraph 3 — the honesty paragraph.** Combining explanations has been proposed before
[7], and rank fusion is standard in information retrieval, where reciprocal-rank fusion is a
strong and very simple baseline [6]. So we claim no priority for the idea of aggregation and
we use both of those rules as baselines. What is new is stated in one sentence: weighting the
two explainers by their **measured** fidelity rather than equally, fitting that weight on a
disjoint calibration split so the rule cannot be tuned on its own test set, and testing the
result against the better of the two rather than their mean.

This paragraph is the most important one in the section. If a reviewer thinks we are
claiming to have invented explanation fusion, the paper is in trouble; this paragraph stops
that reading before it starts.

**Paragraph 4 — the gap, sharply.** Several studies contrast SHAP and LIME on tabular and
clinical data [13], [14]. Most vary a single axis, none we surveyed reports a random-ranking
control or states its attribution space, and none pre-registers its hypotheses. Three
specific, checkable defects — which is exactly what our design fixes.

## 7. Section III — Method

The longest section, and the one most likely to be questioned. It has four subsections plus
the traps.

### 7.1 Datasets (Section III-A)

| Dataset | Rows | Features | Positive class | Nested-CV ROC-AUC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PIMA Indian Diabetes | 768 | 8 | 34.9% | 0.83 |
| UCI Heart Disease (Cleveland subset) | 303 | 13 | 45.9% | 0.90 |
| UCI Chronic Kidney Disease | 400 | 24 | 62.5% | 1.00 |

All three are public, de-identified, and redistributable under CC0 or CC BY 4.0, and all
three are vendored into the repository with SHA-256 checksums verified on every run. The
paper states these numbers in prose because it has room for only one table; the full
dataset table is in the long-form report.

Two things in this subsection are design decisions, not housekeeping:

- **The difficulty gradient.** The three AUCs — 0.83, 0.90, 1.00 — are why we use three
  datasets rather than one. A single-dataset study cannot ask whether agreement depends on
  how hard the decision boundary is. Ours can, and the answer turned out to be the opposite
  of what anyone would guess.
- **Dataset-specific semantics fixes.** In PIMA, zeros in glucose, blood pressure, skin
  thickness, insulin and BMI are biologically impossible, so they are recoded as missing —
  but zeros in *pregnancies* are legitimate and are kept. In Heart, the target is binarised
  at greater than 0, and its nominal codes are carried as categories so the linear model
  cannot read a false ordering into them. Getting either of these wrong changes every number
  downstream.

### 7.2 The six models (Section III-A)

Logistic regression, a decision tree, a random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and a stacking
ensemble — chosen to span linear, single-tree, bagged, boosted and stacked families. Six
models times three datasets is the 18 "cells" the paper keeps referring to.

Stacking is in the list for a specific reason worth remembering: it is the one family with
**no** tree-specific explainer, so SHAP has to fall back to a sampling-based method there.
That makes the stacking cells the only place where SHAP's own re-run variance is a
non-trivial measurement, and it is also the concrete answer to "what is LIME's
model-agnosticism actually worth?"

### 7.3 Two tracks, and the leak they prevent (Section III-A)

- **Track A** measures how good the models are: nested cross-validation, five outer folds
  for estimation and three inner folds for tuning, with every transformer (imputation,
  scaling) inside the pipeline so it is fitted on inner-training rows only.
- **Track B** measures the explanations: it takes Track A's chosen hyperparameters, refits
  once on a stratified 80/20 split, and runs every explainer against that single model per
  cell.

The reason for the separation is one specific leak: **the data partition the explainers see
must never have taken part in tuning.** If it had, the explanations would be describing a
model that had already seen those rows, and every fidelity number would be optimistic.

We also deliberately apply **no** synthetic resampling (no SMOTE and no oversampling). The
imbalance is mild, and oversampling would change the data manifold that LIME's sampler and
SHAP's background both draw from — which would confound the very comparison under study.

### 7.4 Explainers and the control (Section III-B)

SHAP uses TreeExplainer for the four tree models and a model-agnostic permutation sampler
for logistic regression and stacking. LIME uses `LimeTabularExplainer` with the one-hot
columns declared categorical. And a **uniformly random ranking is included as a control.**

The random control is small in the text and large in the argument. An AOPC of 0.260 means
nothing on its own — it is only meaningful next to the 0.075 a random ranking scores on the
same data. Any comparison without a control is reporting numbers whose scale is unknown, and
that is one of the three defects we accuse the prior work of.

### 7.5 The hybrid consensus method (Section III-C, Algorithm 1)

Both tools produce a ranking over the same features, so they can be combined at the level of
ranks without either being rescaled. Algorithm 1 lists three rules:

| Rule | Formula | Whose |
|---|---|---|
| Mean-rank (Borda) | average the two normalised ranks | published baseline |
| Reciprocal-rank fusion | each list contributes 1/(60 + its rank), then rank the totals | published baseline [6] |
| **Fidelity-weighted** | w × (SHAP ranks) + (1 − w) × (LIME ranks) | **ours** |

RRF is in there because it is deliberately robust: the 1/(60 + r) form means one badly wrong
ranking drags the result down less than a plain average would.

Our rule sets the weight from measured faithfulness:

> w = AOPC(SHAP, calibration) / [ AOPC(SHAP, calibration) + AOPC(LIME, calibration) ]

so whichever tool moves the model's prediction more on held-out instances carries more of the
fused ranking. On our data w works out near 0.54 — SHAP slightly ahead.

Two design details carry most of the credibility:

1. **The weight is estimated once per cell, on a calibration split of 30% of the explained
   instances, and RQ6 is tested on the remaining 70% only.** A weight fitted on the same
   instances the hypothesis is tested on would make the rule win by construction. The split
   is recorded per cell, so the separation is auditable rather than asserted.
2. **The bar is the better of the two, not their average.** Beating the average of SHAP and
   LIME is trivial — any sensible combination does it. Beating the winner is the only test
   that means anything, and we set it before running.

### 7.6 The metrics (end of Section III-C)

Written as: let φ(x) be the attribution vector and π(x) the ranking by absolute value.

| Research question | What we measure | How |
|---|---|---|
| RQ1 agreement | do the two rank features the same way? | Spearman ρ, Kendall τ_b, top-k Jaccard, rank-biased overlap |
| RQ2 stability | three separate things | re-run variance over R = 10 seeds; local robustness from m = 5 Gaussian neighbours at 0.1σ; retraining stability over B = 10 bootstrap refits |
| RQ3 fidelity | does the ranking actually drive the model? | AOPC: delete the top-k features (replaced by the training median) and average the fall in the predicted probability, K = min(d, 10); plus insertion AUC, comprehensiveness and sufficiency [9], and a marginal-draw baseline as a sensitivity check |
| RQ4 cost | how expensive is it? | single-threaded wall-clock seconds around the explainer call |
| RQ5 plausibility | does it match what the literature says matters? | top-5 overlap with a reference set frozen before any explanation was computed |
| RQ6 consensus | does fusion beat the better single tool? | AOPC of the fused ranking against the better of the two, evaluation instances only |

One subtlety worth being able to explain, because it looks like a mistake otherwise:
**rank-biased overlap has a floor that depends on how many features you have.** Because both
rankings permute the *same* feature set, RBO's extrapolation term cannot go below 0.589 when
d = 8, but can reach down to 0.193 when d = 24. Raw RBO is therefore not comparable across
our three datasets, so we report a floor-normalised variant as well. We found this with a
test, and it is in the protocol's amendment log.

### 7.7 The four traps (Section III-D)

Each of these silently invalidates a SHAP-versus-LIME comparison, and none of them shows up
in the output when it goes wrong. This subsection is the single strongest part of the paper
and it is worth being able to recite.

**Trap 1 — the two methods must explain the same quantity.** LIME explains predicted
probability. TreeExplainer on boosted models returns log-odds margins *by default*. Compare
those directly and you are contrasting different quantities on different scales, and the
comparison is meaningless. We require probability-space attributions and then **verify** it
by checking that base value + Σφ reproduces `predict_proba`. Median additivity error across
cells is 3.5e-8. In 1 of the 18 cells the pre-registered gate did not pass — PIMA/LightGBM,
where 5 of 150 instances exceed the 5e-3 tolerance, worst 0.094, against a cell median of
1.1e-9. We report that rather than hide it; Section VI says how it is handled.

**Trap 2 — the reference distribution must be real data.** The convenient way to summarise
SHAP's background is k-means, but averaging one-hot columns produces fractional values the
model has never seen, so the "reference" rows are synthetic. Interventional TreeSHAP
evaluated against off-manifold references violates its own additivity: we measured 9.9e-3 on
Heart/LightGBM that way, against 7e-9 with real training rows. So our background is a
seeded, class-stratified subsample of actual rows — which also keeps tree SHAP deterministic.

**Trap 3 — one-hot columns must be aggregated before ranking.** A categorical feature
expanded into several columns fragments across the model's inputs. Left unaggregated,
rankings from different models would be over different vocabularies, and deleting "the top
five features" would delete five *columns* — under-perturbing a 13-feature dataset spread
over 25 columns while leaving an 8-feature one untouched. So we sum the attributions over
each feature's columns before ranking, and perturbation operates on whole feature groups.

**Trap 4 — there has to be a random control.** Covered in 7.4 above: without it, no fidelity
number has a known scale.

### 7.8 Statistics (Section III-E)

Eleven hypotheses were registered in advance, each with its test, its alternative
(one-sided or two-sided), its unit of analysis, and its family. Four choices in this
subsection are the ones a statistically-minded examiner will probe:

- **All tests are non-parametric.** We do not assume the metrics are normally distributed,
  because there is no reason to think they are.
- **p-values are Holm-corrected within each family**, over the tests actually performed.
  Degenerate cells are kept and reported but excluded from the divisor, so the correction is
  not quietly inflated.
- **Every test carries an effect size and every estimate a 95% bootstrap interval.** The
  stated reason is worth quoting: with hundreds of instances per cell, significance is cheap
  and the effect size is the finding.
- **RQ1 is tested against ρ > 0.50, not against zero.** Testing whether two feature rankings
  correlate better than *not at all* would be a straw man. The 0.50 threshold was fixed
  before any ρ was computed.

## 8. Section IV — Results

Table I collects the decision for all eleven hypotheses; each subsection then states the
finding, the effect size and the interval. What follows is one plain-language paragraph per
research question.

### 8.1 RQ1 — substantial agreement, but not interchangeable (Section IV-A)

Median per-instance rank agreement is **ρ = 0.74** with **top-5 Jaccard 0.67**, clearing the
pre-registered bar of ρ > 0.50 in **15 of 18** cells.

How to read Jaccard 0.67: it means the two top-five lists share four features and differ on
one. (Four shared out of six distinct features across the two lists gives 4/6 ≈ 0.67.) So
the honest summary is *"they agree on four of five and disagree on the fifth"* — not "they
agree a third of the time" and not "they are interchangeable". One feature in five is not a
margin a practitioner can ignore, and it is not chaos either.

Then the finding nobody expects. Agreement does **not** follow task difficulty in the
direction you would guess:

| Dataset | Agreement (ρ) | How hard the task is (ROC-AUC) |
|---|---|---|
| PIMA | 0.76 | 0.83 — hardest |
| Heart | 0.78 | 0.90 |
| Chronic Kidney Disease | **0.63 — lowest** | **1.00 — easiest** |

The explanation we favour is redundancy, not difficulty: on CKD several features are each
individually sufficient to decide the case, so two explainers can both be locally faithful
while nominating different ones. If a feature is one of four equally good answers, which one
gets ranked first is close to arbitrary.

### 8.2 RQ2 — reproducible is not the same as robust (Section IV-B)

This is the subsection to lead with if we are allowed only one finding.

| The three things people all call "stability" | SHAP | LIME |
|---|---|---|
| **Re-run variance** — same model, same data, run the tool again | exactly 0 with a tree backend (bit-identical) | median coefficient of variation 0.68 |
| **Input perturbation** — nudge the input slightly | local Lipschitz 0.18 | 0.65 (SHAP better in 15 of 18 cells) |
| **Retraining** — refit the model on a resample of the same data | cross-refit rank agreement 0.83 | 0.76 |

Read across that table and the point falls out. Tree SHAP scores a perfect zero on the first
row, so **a study that measured only re-run variance would have called SHAP perfectly stable
and stopped there.** But its ranking still moves when the model underneath is refitted on a
bootstrap resample of the same data. The instability lives in the model, not in the
explainer, and no amount of determinism in the attribution method removes it.

Also worth knowing: LIME's re-run variance shrinks as its sampling budget grows. So LIME's
reproducibility is a budget the user chooses to pay for, not a property they are given.

### 8.3 RQ3 and RQ4 — SHAP is both more faithful and cheaper (Section IV-C)

**Faithfulness.** Median AOPC is **0.260 for SHAP**, **0.224 for LIME**, against **0.075 for
the random control.** Both explainers beat the control in **36 of 36** tested comparisons
after Holm correction. Deleting the features an explainer puts first collapses the
prediction; deleting a random few barely moves it. That contrast is exactly why an AOPC value
cannot be read without a control.

The marginal-baseline sensitivity check does not reverse the ordering — which matters,
because median replacement pushes instances off the data manifold [10], and if the ordering
had flipped under a different baseline we would have had to say the fidelity result was an
artefact of how we delete features.

**Cost.** Median 0.0019 s per explanation for SHAP against 0.0462 s for LIME — a factor of
about **24.8**. The gap is structural, not an implementation detail: TreeSHAP exploits the
tree structure directly, while LIME has to evaluate the model on thousands of perturbed rows.

Put together: on these models SHAP is simultaneously the more faithful and the cheaper
option, so LIME's case rests on model-agnosticism rather than on measured quality — and our
stacking cells, which admit no tree explainer, are where you see what that is worth.

### 8.4 RQ5 — the plausibility check does not discriminate (Section IV-D)

Overlap with the pre-registered reference feature sets is similar for both tools. Both
clearly beat the random control, but they do not separate from each other.

We report this as a null finding **about the metric**, not about the methods, and we said in
advance that RQ5 was the weakest measurement in the study. Three reasons: reference sets
compiled from the literature are not ground truth; they are incomplete; and top-5 overlap on
an 8-feature dataset has very little room to vary in the first place. Being able to say "we
knew this one was weak before we ran it, and here is where we wrote that down" is worth more
than the result itself.

### 8.5 RQ6 — fusion does not clear the bar (Section IV-E)

Rank fusion, **including our own fidelity-weighted rule**, improved on the better of SHAP and
LIME in **0 of 18** cells, with a median rank-biserial effect of **−0.80**. The best rule
reaches median AOPC 0.248 against SHAP's 0.260 alone.

The important part is that we can say *why*, and that the why follows from RQ1. Fusion pays
off when its inputs are each noisy but wrong in *uncorrelated* ways. Here neither condition
holds:

1. The two tools agree substantially (ρ = 0.74), so there is little independent signal left
   to recover by combining them.
2. One of them is consistently better, so averaging its ranks with a weaker and largely
   redundant signal can only dilute it.

Our fidelity-weighted rule was designed to counter exactly problem 2, and it *was* usually
the best of the three fusion rules — but a weight fitted per cell on a small calibration
sample cannot recover a gap of that size. The conclusion we draw is a conditional one:
explanation aggregation needs **complementary** base explainers, and SHAP and LIME on tree
models are not complementary enough.

## 10. Section V — Discussion

Four paragraphs, and each one turns a number into advice.

**What ρ = 0.74 means for practice.** The two methods are not the wildly divergent pair some
of the disagreement literature implies — but one feature in each top-five list still differs.
So reading the *intersection* of the two lists is **not** a robustness check: it silently
drops a feature from each explanation. And if a decision turns on which features appear in a
top-five list, then the choice of explainer is part of that decision and belongs in the
record. That last clause is the paper's one actionable recommendation for anyone deploying
this in practice.

**Which one to use.** On this evidence the default should be SHAP wherever a tree backend
exists: across the 18 cells it wins on every axis we measured except model-agnosticism. We
then say that the remaining axis is precisely LIME's case, and that it is not a small one. Do
not skip that concession when presenting — it is what keeps the recommendation credible.

**The result we would most want carried forward.** Tree SHAP's re-run variance is exactly
zero, so a study measuring stability that way alone reports it as perfectly stable; its
cross-refit agreement was 0.83, meaning the global ranking moves when the model is refitted
on a resample of the same training data. **The instability lives in the model, not the
explainer**, and no amount of determinism in the attribution method removes it. The
recommendation: anyone reporting explanation stability should say which of the three things
they measured.

**Freezing the protocol repaid itself three times.** This paragraph is the argument for
pre-registration made with evidence rather than principle. Three bugs were caught by tests
that existed only because the protocol demanded them:

1. The additivity check caught a background-construction error that violated SHAP's own
   additivity by about one per cent on LightGBM cells, while every other cell looked healthy.
2. A test requiring each interval to contain its own point estimate caught a misapplication
   of the BCa bootstrap to a non-smooth statistic.
3. A test on the RBO floor caught that raw RBO is not comparable across datasets of different
   width.

None of the three would have been visible in the results. All three are recorded in the
protocol's amendment log with dates and reasons.

## 11. Section VI — Limitations and Threats to Validity

The section opens with *"We state these before a reader has to raise them,"* which is the
whole strategy. Six limitations, each named in bold in the paper:

| Limitation | What it means | How we handle it |
|---|---|---|
| **Three datasets, all small and all clinical** | generalisation across *datasets* rests on three points | the per-cell tests have real power; the difficulty gradient is a design compensation, and we call it a compensation rather than a substitute for breadth |
| **Perturbation baselines are off-manifold** | replacing a feature with the median creates rows the real process would not produce [10] | we ran a marginal-draw variant as a check and it did not reverse the ordering — though both share an independence assumption |
| **The plausibility reference sets are not ground truth** | they are literature-derived, incomplete, non-causal, and shaped by what their sources chose to measure | which is exactly why RQ5 is reported as corroborating evidence only, and why we said so in advance |
| **Retraining stability covers three of six models** | not all six | restricted in advance on compute grounds, not chosen after seeing results |
| **Timing is single-machine** | the 24.8× gap is hardware-specific in magnitude | but structural in origin — tree traversal versus thousands of model evaluations |
| **No human evaluation** | every metric is a property of the model–explanation pair, not of a person's understanding | so the paper claims nothing about human comprehension and does not over-reach |

The section closes with a sentence worth memorising: *a faithful post-hoc explanation of a
black box is still not an interpretable model.*

### 11.1 The failed additivity gate — read this one carefully

The second half of Section VI is the part we should be proudest of and the part most likely to
be probed.

In PIMA/LightGBM, 5 of 150 explained instances reconstruct `predict_proba` to worse than the
5e-3 tolerance (worst error 0.094) against a cell median of 1.1e-9. So the pre-registered
additivity rule **is not satisfied** for that cell.

We had written down two options in advance: force that cell into log-odds space, which would
remove it from every cross-explainer comparison, or widen the gate. **We did neither.**
Widening a gate after seeing the result it fails is precisely what pre-registration exists to
prevent. Instead the cell is retained, the failure is stated in the paper, and every tally is
additionally reported with that cell excluded — and no decision in Table I changes either way.

We also tried to attribute the discrepancy and could not: it is insensitive to background
size, and the obvious candidate (those instances coinciding with a median-imputed value) was
tested and rejected. Saying "we do not know why" is part of the finding, not a gap in it.

## 12. Section VII — Conclusion

Five sentences, and they are deliberately the same five claims as the abstract, in the same
order. Nothing new appears in a conclusion.

1. Across 18 model–dataset cells, SHAP and LIME agree substantially (median ρ = 0.74) without
   being interchangeable: at the median, one of the five features in each top-five list
   differs.
2. Where a tree backend exists, SHAP is the more faithful, roughly 24.8× cheaper, and markedly
   more perturbation-robust.
3. Explanation stability separates into three sources, and the one a deterministic explainer
   cannot address — variance from retraining the model — is the one that persists.
4. Rank-level fusion did not clear the pre-registered bar in any cell.
5. And that follows from the combination: substantial agreement leaves little complementary
   signal, and a consistent advantage can only be diluted by averaging.

If someone asks "what is the one-sentence takeaway?", it is sentence 3.

## 13. Ethics and Reproducibility

This is the only starred section left at the back of the paper. Two paragraphs.

**Ethics.** Three public, de-identified, redistributable benchmark datasets; no human
subjects; no clinical deployment. The diseases are benchmarks for a computer-science question.
The paper says explicitly that no model trained here is fit for diagnostic use and that no
feature ranking should be read as a clinical or causal statement. This paragraph exists so
that nobody can read a medical claim into the paper, and we should be able to say that in one
breath if asked.

**Reproducibility.** Everything in one paragraph:

- every number, figure and table is generated by code, and none is transcribed by hand;
- dependencies are pinned to exact versions;
- the source datasets are vendored with SHA-256 checksums verified on every run;
- a manifest records a digest for every artefact, alongside the git commit, the seed set, the
  resolved library versions, and the hardware;
- the analysis protocol was committed **in its own commit, before any commit containing
  results** — so the repository history is itself the evidence that the hypotheses were fixed
  in advance;
- all later changes to the protocol appear in its amendment log with dates and reasons.

That third-from-last point is the strongest sentence in the paper's defence. Anyone can *say*
they pre-registered; we can point at the commit graph.

## 14. References — why each one is there

### 14.1 How the list was chosen

Our supervisor asked for a shorter list — around 16 or 17 items — with the older work dropped.
We went from 27 citations to **17**. The rule we applied was not "keep the newest": it was

> **keep a reference if removing it would leave a claim, a metric, or a dataset unsupported.**

That rule is what a reviewer would apply, and it is why a few genuinely old references
survived while some newer ones were cut. Concretely, a reference stays if it is one of:

1. the paper that defines one of the two tools we study;
2. the paper that defines a metric we report (you cannot use a named metric and not cite it);
3. the source of one of the three datasets;
4. one of the two published fusion rules we use as baselines;
5. the prior work that our novelty claim has to be honest about;
6. the prior comparison that establishes the gap we are filling;
7. the legal or empirical motivation for the problem existing at all.

Everything that was only *nice to have* went.

### 14.2 The 17 references, one at a time

The numbers are the numbers as printed in the current build. IEEE numbers references in
order of *first citation*, so if anyone reorders the introduction the numbers shift — check
against `paper/main.pdf` before quoting a number out loud.

**[1] EU AI Act — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, 2024.**
The legal reason this problem matters. It obliges providers of high-risk AI systems to make
their operation interpretable. *Why we kept it:* it turns "interpretability is interesting"
into "interpretability is required", which is a much stronger opening. It is also our most
recent reference.

**[2] Lundberg and Lee, "A unified approach to interpreting model predictions," NeurIPS 2017.**
The SHAP paper. Defines the method and proves the uniqueness of the Shapley-value attribution
under its axioms. *Why we kept it:* it is impossible to write about SHAP without it. This is
reference #1 in the "cannot be cut" category.

**[3] Lundberg et al., "From local explanations to global understanding with explainable AI for
trees," Nature Machine Intelligence 2020.**
TreeSHAP / TreeExplainer — the exact, polynomial-time algorithm for tree ensembles. *Why we
kept it separately from [2]:* [2] introduces SHAP in general, but our claim that SHAP is
*exact and deterministic on trees* — which is the basis of the whole RQ2 finding — comes from
this paper, not from [2]. Dropping it would leave that claim mis-attributed.

**[4] Ribeiro, Singh and Guestrin, "Why should I trust you?", KDD 2016.**
The LIME paper. *Why we kept it:* same reason as [2] — it is one of the two objects of study.

**[5] Slack et al., "Fooling LIME and SHAP," AIES 2020.**
Shows that both methods can be deliberately fooled by an adversary. *Why we kept it:* it
supports two separate sentences — that the two tools' failure modes are unrelated (Section I)
and that neither should be taken on trust (Section II). It is also the citation that justifies
our stance of "careful measurement rather than settled verdicts."

**[6] Cormack, Clarke and Buettcher, "Reciprocal rank fusion outperforms Condorcet and
individual rank learning methods," SIGIR 2009.**
Reciprocal-rank fusion (RRF). *Why we kept it even though it is from 2009:* RRF is one of the
two baselines we actually run, and the 1/(60 + rank) form in Algorithm 1 is taken from this
paper. We are using their method; we have to cite them. Cited three times.

**[7] Bhatt, Weller and Moura, "Evaluating and aggregating feature-based model explanations,"
IJCAI 2020.**
Prior work on combining explanations. *Why we kept it:* this is the honesty citation. We
claim a hybrid method of our own, so the paper has to say clearly that *aggregating
explanations is not our idea*. This reference is what that sentence points at. Removing it would turn a
careful novelty claim into an overclaim, which is the fastest way to lose a reviewer.

**[8] Samek et al., "Evaluating the visualization of what a deep neural network has learned,"
IEEE TNNLS 2017.**
The origin of the deletion/perturbation curve summarised as area over the perturbation curve —
i.e. AOPC. *Why we kept it:* AOPC is our headline fidelity metric and the quantity our own
fusion weight is computed from. Using it without citing its source would be a real omission.

**[9] DeYoung et al., "ERASER: A benchmark to evaluate rationalized NLP models," ACL 2020.**
Formalised **comprehensiveness** and **sufficiency**. *Why we kept it:* we report both of
those metrics alongside AOPC, under those names, with those definitions.

**[10] Hooker et al., "A benchmark for interpretability methods in deep neural networks,"
NeurIPS 2019.**
Shows that deletion-style tests are confounded, because deleting features produces inputs the
data-generating process would never produce ("off-manifold"). *Why we kept it:* it is cited
three times and it is load-bearing every time — it is the reason we run a marginal-draw
sensitivity check at all, and it is the source of one of our stated limitations. A reviewer who
knows this literature will look for exactly this citation, and its absence would read as not
knowing about the problem.

**[11] Alvarez-Melis and Jaakkola, "On the robustness of interpretability methods," 2018.**
Defines the local Lipschitz estimate of explanation robustness. *Why we kept it:* that estimate
is the entire input-perturbation axis of RQ2 — the 0.18 versus 0.65 numbers come from this
definition.

**[12] Webber, Moffat and Zobel, "A similarity measure for indefinite rankings," ACM TOIS 2010.**
Defines rank-biased overlap (RBO). *Why we kept it despite being from 2010:* we report RBO, and
we also report a floor-normalised variant of it — and you cannot explain why a floor exists
without the original definition. Another case of "using their method, so citing them."

**[13] Salih et al., "A perspective on explainable artificial intelligence methods: SHAP and
LIME," Advanced Intelligent Systems 2024.**
The most recent direct treatment of exactly our two methods. *Why we kept it:* it establishes
that the SHAP-versus-LIME question is live and current, which is what justifies the paper
existing in 2026. Together with [14] it carries the "prior comparisons hold too much fixed"
argument.

**[14] Duell et al., "A comparison of explanations given by explainable artificial intelligence
methods on analysing electronic health records," IEEE BHI 2021.**
The closest prior work in our own domain: comparing explainers on clinical records. *Why we
kept it:* it is the nearest neighbour to what we did, so leaving it out would look like we had
not found it. It is also the reference that makes "on tabular **and clinical** data" in Section
II true rather than loose.

**[15] Smith et al., "Using the ADAP learning algorithm to forecast the onset of diabetes
mellitus," 1988.**
The source of the PIMA Indian Diabetes dataset. *Why we kept a 1988 reference:* it is where the
data came from. Data provenance is not optional, and citing a dataset to its origin rather than
to a download page is the correct form.

**[16] Detrano et al., "International application of a new probability algorithm for the
diagnosis of coronary artery disease," Am. J. Cardiology 1989.**
The source of the Cleveland Heart Disease dataset. Same reasoning as [15].

**[17] Rubini, Soundarapandian and Eswaran, "Chronic kidney disease," UCI Machine Learning
Repository 2015.**
The source of the CKD dataset. Same reasoning as [15].

## 16. Questions we are likely to be asked

**"Your own method failed. How is that a contribution?"**
Because the study was designed so that a negative answer is informative. The bar, the
calibration split and the hypothesis were all fixed in advance and recorded in version
control, so "fusion does not beat the better single explainer here" is a measured result
rather than a failed attempt. And we can say *why* it failed and under what condition it
would work: fusion needs base explainers whose errors are uncorrelated, and SHAP and LIME on
tree models agree too much for that. A reader can act on that.

**"Your method is not in the title. Are you hiding the negative result?"**
No, and the paper is the evidence: it is contribution 3 in Section I, hypothesis RQ6 in
Table I, its own results subsection (IV-E), a discussion paragraph in Section V, and a plain
sentence in the abstract. What the title reflects is proportion. The hybrid is one of six
research questions; the other five are the comparison, and the comparison is what the paper
is for. See §1.3 for the full history of the title.

**"Why did you remove the numbers from the abstract?"**
Our supervisor asked for it, and the reasoning holds: an abstract dense with decimals is hard
to read and impossible to remember, and every number is in Section IV. The one that stayed —
about 24.8 times faster — is the single most concrete, most actionable number in the paper.

**"Why is the paper only six pages when the report is 47?"**
Six pages including references is the conference limit. The paper is the argument; the report
is the full record, with the complete per-cell tables and all the figures. Both are built from
the same generated numbers, so they cannot disagree.

**"How do we know you did not decide the hypotheses after seeing the results?"**
Because `docs/PROTOCOL.md` was committed in its own commit, before any commit that contains a
result. The git history is the proof, and anyone can check it. When we did change something,
it went into the protocol's amendment log with a date and a reason — and the one place where a
pre-registered gate failed (PIMA/LightGBM additivity) is reported as a failure rather than
smoothed over.

**"Which of you can answer questions about this?"**
All of us should be able to answer sections 1 through 4 and 15 of this file. Beyond that,
`docs/CONTRIBUTIONS.md` records who owned which part, and Part 13 of
[EXPLANATION_GUIDE.md](EXPLANATION_GUIDE.md) suggests who takes which question.

**"What would you do next?"**
The honest answer, and it is in the paper's own logic: test fusion where the base explainers
are genuinely complementary — different model families, or an explainer that is not
attribution-based — rather than two tools that agree on four features out of five. Also more
datasets, and a human evaluation, which this study deliberately does not attempt.
