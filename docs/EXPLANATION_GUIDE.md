## 1. The project in plain words

### 1.1 One paragraph

When a machine-learning model decides something about a person — that a patient probably
has diabetes, say — the model cannot tell you *why*. Two tools dominate the job of
explaining such a decision after the fact: **SHAP** and **LIME**. They are built on
completely different mathematics, and when you run both on the same prediction they often
disagree about which features mattered. Practitioners have no principled way to decide
which one to believe. This thesis does two things about that. **First**, it compares the
two properly — same data, same models, same metric definitions, same statistical
treatment — across 6 classifiers × 3 public medical datasets = **18 combinations** and
**1,746 individually explained predictions**. **Second**, it proposes a method for
*combining* them, **fidelity-weighted rank fusion**, which gives more weight to whichever
explainer proves more faithful on held-out data. The comparison produced clear answers.
The combination did not work — and we can explain exactly why it did not, which is a
finding in itself.

### 1.2 The three contributions (memorise these)

1. **A reproducible multi-axis benchmark.** Two explainers, three fusion rules, and a
   random-ranking control, across six classifiers and three datasets, under one
   leakage-safe protocol, with eleven hypotheses written down *before* any result existed.
2. **A three-way decomposition of explanation stability.** Explainer randomness, input
   perturbation, and model-retraining variance are three different things. Studies that
   report one "stability" number conflate them, and the distinction changes the conclusion.
3. **A hybrid consensus method, and an honest account of its limits.** Fidelity-weighted
   rank fusion is ours; it is tested against two published fusion baselines and against a
   deliberately strict bar. It failed to clear that bar, the null outcome was
   pre-registered as valid, and we explain the mechanism.

### 1.3 The title, decoded

> Benchmark on Clinical Tabular Data**

## 2. The vocabulary, explained once

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Tabular data** | an ordinary table: rows are patients, columns are measurements. Not images, not text. |
| **Classifier** | a model that outputs a class, here "disease" or "no disease", with a probability. |
| **Black box** | a model whose reasoning you cannot read off its parameters. Tree ensembles are the standard example, and they are also the most accurate models on tabular data. |
| **Post-hoc explanation** | an explanation produced *after* the model is trained, by a separate tool, without changing the model. |
| **Feature attribution** | a number per feature saying how much it pushed this one prediction. Written φ (phi). |
| **Ranking / top-5 list** | order the features by the size of φ, ignoring sign, biggest first. The top-5 list is what a practitioner actually reads. |
| **Cell** | one (dataset, model) pair. 3 datasets × 6 models = **18 cells**. The word appears constantly in the paper. |
| **Instance** | one row = one patient = one prediction we explained. 1,746 of them. |

### 2.1 SHAP, in one idea

Imagine a team wins a prize and you must split it fairly among the players. Game theory
gives a unique fair answer, the **Shapley value**: for each player, average their marginal
contribution over every possible order in which the players could have joined the team.
SHAP treats *features* as the players and the *prediction* as the prize. For tree models,
`TreeExplainer` computes this exactly and quickly by exploiting the tree structure, so
**SHAP on trees is deterministic** — run it twice, get bit-identical numbers.

### 2.2 LIME, in one idea

LIME does not care about the model globally. It takes one row, generates thousands of
slightly altered copies of it, asks the model for a prediction on each, weights those
copies by how close they are to the original, and fits a **simple straight-line model** to
that local cloud. The straight line's coefficients are the explanation. Because the copies
are drawn at random, **LIME is stochastic** — run it twice, get slightly different numbers.

### 2.3 Why they can disagree

They are answering different questions. SHAP attributes the model's *output*; LIME
approximates the model's *local function*. On top of that, LIME samples randomly, and its
answer depends on the sampling budget you give it. So two different top-5 lists is not a
bug in either tool — it is what you should expect. The open question is what to do about it.

## 3. Why the work was needed

The best models on tabular data are unreadable, they are being deployed where decisions
affect people, and regulation now requires that their operation be interpretable (the EU AI
Act obliges this for high-risk systems). So explanations are not optional. But if a
practitioner runs SHAP and LIME and gets two different answers, nothing in the literature
tells them which to trust.

Prior comparisons do not settle it because **they hold too much fixed**:

- typically one model family, or one dataset — so you cannot tell whether a finding is
  about the explainer or about that particular model;
- "fidelity" defined differently from paper to paper — so results cannot be compared;
- no hypothesis tests, no effect sizes;
- **no random-ranking control** — so a fidelity score has no scale;
- and none states which *output space* the attributions live in (probability or log-odds),
  which quietly invalidates the comparison (see the four traps, Part 8).

> **The sentence to say:** *the gap is not a missing algorithm, it is a missing controlled
> comparison — and that is something an experiment can close.*

## 4. What we built: the hybrid method

This is the part the title leads with, so be ready to explain it without notes.

### 4.1 The idea

SHAP and LIME produce numbers on different scales, so you cannot simply average their
attributions. But both end up producing an **ordered list of the same features**. Ranks are
directly comparable, so we combine at the rank level — no rescaling, no assumption that the
two numeric scales mean the same thing.

### 4.2 Three rules — two borrowed, one ours

| Rule | Formula, in words | Whose it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Mean-rank (Borda)** | average the two normalised ranks | published baseline |
| **Reciprocal-rank fusion (RRF)** | each list contributes 1/(60 + its rank); add and re-rank | published baseline (Cormack et al., standard in search engines) |
| **Fidelity-weighted fusion** | weighted average of the two ranks, where the weight is each explainer's *measured* faithfulness | **ours** |

Why RRF exists at all: because `1/(60+rank)` flattens out down the list, one badly wrong
ranking damages the result less than a plain arithmetic mean would.

### 4.3 What "fidelity-weighted" means, with a worked example

Mean-rank treats the two explainers as equally trustworthy. Ours does not. It asks each
explainer to *earn* its weight:

```
w = AOPC_shap / (AOPC_shap + AOPC_lime)        ← measured on the calibration split
fused rank = w × (SHAP's rank) + (1 − w) × (LIME's rank)
```

AOPC is the faithfulness score defined in Part 6.3 — how far the model's prediction falls
when you delete the features an explainer put first. So the explainer whose ranking actually
moves the model carries more of the fused rank.

A toy cell where SHAP scores 0.26 and LIME 0.22 on calibration gives
`w = 0.26 / 0.48 = 0.54`:

| Feature | SHAP rank | LIME rank | Mean-rank | Fidelity-weighted (w = 0.54) | Fused order |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glucose | 1 | 2 | 1.5 | 0.54·1 + 0.46·2 = **1.46** | 1st |
| BMI | 3 | 1 | 2.0 | 0.54·3 + 0.46·1 = **2.08** | 2nd |
| Age | 2 | 4 | 3.0 | 0.54·2 + 0.46·4 = **2.92** | 3rd |
| Insulin | 4 | 3 | 3.5 | 0.54·4 + 0.46·3 = **3.54** | 4th |

Note what the weighting did: mean-rank puts BMI ahead of Age purely on the average, while
fidelity weighting leans towards SHAP's opinion because SHAP earned the larger weight on
this cell's calibration data. The weight is estimated **once per cell**, not globally,
because faithfulness differs from cell to cell.

### 4.4 The honesty guard: a disjoint calibration split

The weight `w` is fitted on **30 % of the explained instances**, and RQ6 is then tested on
the remaining **70 % only**. Those two sets never overlap.

This matters more than it looks. If we had fitted `w` on the same rows we later tested on,
the rule would win *by construction* — it would have been tuned on its own test set, and a
victory would prove nothing. The split is recorded per cell in
`results/consensus_splits.csv`, so the separation is auditable rather than merely asserted.

> If the advisor asks one technical question about the hybrid, this is the likeliest one.
> The answer is: **the weight is fitted on data the hypothesis is not tested on.**

### 4.5 The bar we set

A fused ranking must beat **the better of SHAP and LIME in the same cell** — not their
average, and not the weaker of the two.

Why so strict: beating the average is trivial. Any sensible blend of a strong signal and a
weak one lands above the midpoint of the two, so "we beat the mean of SHAP and LIME" would
be a result with no practical content. A practitioner would only adopt a hybrid if it beat
the best option they already have. That is the bar, and it was fixed in the protocol before
we ran anything.

## 5. The experiment

### 5.1 Three datasets, chosen for a difficulty gradient

| Dataset | Rows | Features | Positive rate | ROC-AUC | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PIMA Indian Diabetes | 768 | 8 | 34.9 % | 0.83 | hard task |
| Heart Disease (Cleveland) | 303 | 13 | 45.9 % | 0.90 | medium task |
| Chronic Kidney Disease | 400 | 24 | 62.5 % | 1.00 | easy task |

All three are public, de-identified, redistributable (CC0 or CC BY 4.0), and vendored in the
repository with SHA-256 checksums that are verified on every run.

**Why three, and why of different difficulty:** so we can ask whether explainer agreement
depends on how hard the decision boundary is. A single-dataset study cannot pose that
question at all. (What we found is more interesting than expected — see Part 7.2.)

**The diseases are benchmarks.** We make no medical claim, no model here is fit for
diagnostic use, and no feature ranking should be read as clinical or causal. Say this
early; it protects the whole talk.

**Cleaning, fixed in advance:** in PIMA, zeros in glucose, blood pressure, skin thickness,
insulin and BMI are biologically impossible and are recoded as missing — but zeros in
*pregnancies* are legitimate and kept. In Heart, the five-level target is binarised at
> 0, and the nominal codes are carried as categories so the linear model cannot read an
ordering into them.

### 5.2 Six models

Logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and a stacking
ensemble — spanning linear → single tree → bagged → boosted → stacked.

**Why stacking is in there:** it is the one family that admits no tree-specific SHAP
backend, so SHAP must fall back to a sampling-based explainer. That makes stacking the only
place where **SHAP's own run-to-run variance is a non-trivial measurement**. It is in the
design for a reason, not for coverage.

Logistic regression and the decision tree are included as interpretable reference points,
not as competitive baselines.

### 5.3 18 cells, 1,746 explained predictions

Up to 150 test instances are explained per cell: PIMA 150 (of 154), Heart 61 (all), CKD 80
(all). So 6 × (150 + 61 + 80) = **1,746**.

### 5.4 Two separated tracks — the leakage guard

| | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Track A** | estimates performance by nested cross-validation: 5 outer folds for the estimate, 3 inner folds for hyperparameter tuning, with every transformer (imputation, scaling, encoding) *inside* the pipeline so it is fitted on inner-training rows only. |
| **Track B** | takes Track A's winning hyperparameters, refits **once** on a single stratified 80/20 split, and runs every explainer against that one model per cell. |

**Why separate them:** explanation analysis needs *one* model per cell, not five fold
models — you cannot explain "the average of five models". And the partition the explainers
see must never have taken part in tuning. Conflating these two things is the standard way
explanation studies leak, and the leak is invisible in the output.

### 5.5 No SMOTE, deliberately

Class imbalance is mild (34.9–62.5 % positive). Synthetic oversampling would alter the data
manifold that LIME's neighbourhood sampler and SHAP's background distribution both draw
from — that is, it would change the very thing under comparison. We use balanced class
weights where the estimator supports them and report MCC and PR-AUC alongside accuracy.
This is recorded in the protocol so it reads as a design decision, not an oversight.

### 5.6 The random control

A uniformly random feature ranking is run as an additional "explainer". It is not padding:
without it, a fidelity score has no scale (Part 8, trap 4).

## 6. The six questions, and how each was measured

| RQ | The question in plain words | Primary metric |
|---|---|---|
| **RQ1** | Do SHAP and LIME rank features the same way, and does that depend on the model or the task? | Spearman ρ per instance |
| **RQ2** | How stable are the explanations — separating three different kinds of stability? | CV; local Lipschitz; cross-refit agreement |
| **RQ3** | Are the explanations actually faithful to what the model does, compared with a random ranking? | AOPC (deletion test) |
| **RQ4** | What does each explanation cost in time, and what faithfulness does that buy? | median seconds per explanation |
| **RQ5** | Do the top features match what the medical literature says matters? | top-5 overlap (Jaccard@5) |
| **RQ6** | Does combining the two beat the better of them alone? | ΔAOPC vs. max(SHAP, LIME) |

### 6.1 Agreement (RQ1)

Four measures, because each has a blind spot: **Spearman ρ** (do the two full orderings
match?), **Kendall τ_b** (same idea, robust to ties), **Jaccard@k** (how much do the top-k
*sets* overlap, for k = 3 and 5), and **rank-biased overlap (RBO)** (top-weighted similarity
that does not require you to pick a k).

One subtlety we found and fixed: because both rankings are permutations of the **same**
feature set, RBO has a hard floor that scales with the number of features. Even a completely
reversed ranking scores 0.589 with 8 features but only 0.193 with 24. So raw RBO is not
comparable across datasets of different width, and we also report a floor-normalised
variant. Worth mentioning — it shows the metrics were understood, not just called.

### 6.2 Stability (RQ2), split into three

This is the decomposition that is contribution 2. Three genuinely different questions:

1. **Re-run variance (2a) — "is the tool reproducible?"** Explain the same row 10 times
   with different random seeds and measure how much the numbers move (coefficient of
   variation).
2. **Local robustness (2b) — "does a tiny change in the patient change the story?"** Draw
   5 neighbours at 0.1 standard deviations around the row, holding categorical columns
   fixed, and take the largest change in explanation per unit change in input (a local
   Lipschitz estimate). Lower is better.
3. **Retraining stability (2c) — "does the story survive refitting the model?"** Refit the
   model on 10 bootstrap resamples of the same training data and measure whether the global
   feature ranking holds up.

**Why this matters:** a study that reports one "stability" number is silently choosing one
of these three, and the choice changes the conclusion completely (Part 7.3). Retraining
stability is run for the three ensemble models only, a restriction decided in advance on
compute grounds.

### 6.3 Fidelity (RQ3) — the deletion test, and AOPC

The idea: if an explanation is faithful, the features it puts *first* should be the ones the
model actually leans on. So:

1. take the explanation's ranking;
2. delete the top-1 feature (replace it with the training median) and re-predict;
3. record how far the model's probability for **its own predicted class** falls;
4. repeat for top-2, top-3, … up to 10 features (or all of them, whichever is fewer);
5. average those falls. That average is **AOPC**.

Higher AOPC = more faithful. We also report insertion AUC, comprehensiveness and
sufficiency, and we re-run the whole thing with a different replacement value (a marginal
draw instead of the median) as a sensitivity check — because replacing with the median
creates rows that the real world would not produce, a known confound.

### 6.4 Cost (RQ4)

Median single-threaded wall-clock seconds around the explainer call. Simple, and honest
about being machine-specific.

### 6.5 Plausibility (RQ5)

Overlap between each explainer's top-5 and a reference list of features that published
clinical literature says matter — compiled with citations and **frozen before any
explanation was computed**. This is corroborating evidence only: literature reference sets
are incomplete and are not ground truth. We said so in advance.

### 6.6 Statistics, in one paragraph

Eleven hypotheses were registered in advance, each with its test, its direction, its unit of
analysis, and its family. All tests are non-parametric — Wilcoxon signed-rank throughout,
except Mann–Whitney U for one and a 10,000-draw permutation test for another. *p*-values are
**Holm-corrected within each family**. Every test carries an **effect size** and every
estimate a **95 % bootstrap confidence interval**, because with hundreds of instances per
cell statistical significance is cheap and the effect size is the actual finding. One
detail worth knowing: RQ1 is tested against ρ > 0.50, not against zero — testing against
zero would be a straw man — and the 0.50 threshold was fixed before any ρ was computed.

## 7. What we found

### 7.1 RQ1 — they agree substantially, but they are not interchangeable

- Median Spearman ρ = **0.74**
- Median top-5 Jaccard = **0.67**
- Cleared the pre-registered bar of ρ > 0.50 in **15 of 18 cells**

**What 0.67 means concretely, because you may be asked:** at the median the two top-5 lists
share **4 of 5 features and differ on 1**. (The paper phrases the disagreement as "roughly a
third of each top-five list", which is the Jaccard *distance*, 1 − 0.67. If someone asks you
to cash that out in features, the answer is "one feature in five at the median, four shared
out of six in the union".)

**Why it matters:** the two agree more than the disagreement literature would lead you to
expect — and still differ enough that running both is **not** a validation check. If you
take only the features both agree on, you silently drop a feature from each list. If a
decision turns on who is in the top five, the choice of explainer is part of that decision
and belongs in the record.

### 7.2 The surprise: agreement is lowest on the *easiest* dataset

| Dataset | ROC-AUC (how easy) | Median ρ (how much they agree) |
|---|---|---|
| PIMA | 0.83 (hard) | 0.76 |
| Heart | 0.90 (medium) | 0.78 |
| CKD | 1.00 (easiest) | **0.63** |

The intuition most people have — harder task, more disagreement — is not what happened.

**Our reading:** what drives agreement is not difficulty but **feature redundancy**. In CKD
several features are individually almost sufficient to separate the classes. When that is
true, two explainers can *both* be locally faithful while nominating different features,
because there are several equally good ways to explain the same prediction.

**Be careful here, and say this yourself:** H1c (does concordance track task difficulty?) was
**not** rejected — adjusted *p* = 0.243. With three datasets there is no statistical power to
test a trend across datasets. So this is a **descriptive observation with a proposed
explanation**, not a proven law. Volunteering that limit is stronger than being caught on it.

### 7.3 RQ2 — "reproducible" is not the same as "robust"

This is the finding we would most want carried forward, and the three numbers only make
sense together:

| Kind of stability | SHAP | LIME | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Re-run (same model, new seed) | **exactly 0** variance | CV 0.68 | SHAP on trees is bit-identical; LIME is not |
| Input perturbation (local Lipschitz, lower better) | **0.18** | 0.65 | SHAP markedly more robust, differing in 15 of 18 cells — but neither is insensitive |
| Model retraining (cross-refit rank agreement) | **0.83** | 0.76 | *both* move when the model is refitted on a resample of the same data |

Two things to be precise about:

- SHAP's zero re-run variance was **declared in the protocol in advance as a property of the
  method**, not presented as a discovery. TreeExplainer is deterministic given a fixed
  background. Claiming it as a finding would be dishonest, and we do not.
- LIME's reproducibility *shrinks with budget* — increase `num_samples` and the variance
  falls. So LIME's reproducibility is a budget the user pays for, not a property they get.

**The point of the decomposition:** a study measuring stability the first way alone would
have recorded SHAP as *perfectly stable* and stopped there. But its cross-refit agreement is
0.83 — the global ranking moves when the model is refitted on a bootstrap resample of the
same training data. **The instability lives in the model, not in the explainer, and no
amount of determinism in the attribution method removes it.** Anyone reporting explanation
stability should say which of the three things they measured.

### 7.4 RQ3 and RQ4 — SHAP is both more faithful and cheaper

| | SHAP | LIME | Random control |
|---|---|---|---|
| AOPC (faithfulness, higher better) | **0.260** | 0.224 | 0.075 |
| Seconds per explanation | **0.0019** | 0.0462 | — |

- Both explainers beat the random control in **36 of 36** tested comparisons after Holm
  correction. That is what makes 0.260 and 0.224 mean anything at all.
- The speed gap is about **24.8×**, and it is **structural, not an implementation detail**:
  TreeSHAP exploits the tree structure, while LIME must evaluate the model on thousands of
  perturbed rows for every single explanation.
- The marginal-baseline sensitivity check does not reverse the ordering, which matters
  because median replacement pushes instances off the data manifold.

**The conclusion to state:** wherever a tree backend exists, SHAP is *simultaneously* the
more faithful and the cheaper option. LIME's remaining case is **model-agnosticism** — it
works on anything — and that is a real advantage, not a small one. Our stacking cells, which
admit no tree explainer, are where you can see what it is worth.

### 7.5 RQ5 — the plausibility check did not discriminate

Both explainers clearly beat the random control on overlap with the literature reference
sets, but they do not separate from each other.

We report this as a **null finding about the metric, not about the methods**: reference sets
compiled from literature are incomplete and are not ground truth, and top-5 overlap on an
8-feature dataset has very little room to vary. RQ5 was pre-registered as the weakest
measurement in the study, and we said so before running it.

### 7.6 RQ6 — the hybrid did not clear the bar

- Fusion beat the better of SHAP and LIME in **0 of 18 cells**
- Median rank-biserial effect **−0.80** — large, and consistently in the wrong direction
- Best fusion rule reaches AOPC **0.248**, against **0.260** for SHAP alone

**Why it failed — say this confidently, it is the strongest part of the answer:**

Fusion pays off when two conditions hold: the inputs are individually noisy, **and** their
errors are uncorrelated. That is the whole ensemble argument. Here **neither** holds.

1. The two explainers **agree substantially** (ρ = 0.74), so there is very little
   independent signal left for fusion to recover.
2. One of them is **consistently better**, so averaging its ranks with a weaker and largely
   redundant signal can only dilute it.

Our fidelity-weighted rule was designed to counter exactly problem 2, by giving the more
faithful explainer more weight, and it was **usually the best of the three rules**. But a
weight fitted per cell on a small calibration sample cannot close the gap.

**The general lesson, which is transferable beyond this thesis:** explanation aggregation
needs **complementary** base explainers. SHAP and LIME on tree models are not complementary
enough. That is a statement about when fusion is worth trying at all, and it is the useful
thing that came out of the negative result.

### 7.7 The eleven hypotheses, in plain language

| H | What it says | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | SHAP/LIME agreement is better than ρ = 0.50 | rejected the null in **15/18** cells |
| H1b | agreement differs between simple models and ensembles | not rejected (p = 0.291) |
| H1c | agreement tracks task difficulty | not rejected (p = 0.243) |
| H2a | LIME's re-run variance is above zero | **18/18** |
| H2b | SHAP and LIME differ in local robustness | **15/18** (SHAP more robust) |
| H2c | SHAP and LIME differ in retraining stability | **9/9** |
| H3a | SHAP and LIME differ in fidelity | **13/18** (SHAP more faithful) |
| H3b | each explainer beats the random control | **36/36** |
| H4 | SHAP costs less than LIME | **15/18** |
| H5 | SHAP and LIME differ in plausibility overlap | not rejected (p = 0.375) — exploratory |
| H6 | fusion beats the better of the two | **0/18** — the pre-registered null result |

"Rejected" always means the *null* hypothesis was rejected at Holm-adjusted p < 0.05 in
that many cells. Four hypotheses were not rejected. Three of them — H1b, H1c, H5 — are
underpowered by design: they compare one value per cell across a handful of cells, so a
null there means "we could not tell", not "there is no effect". H6 is the substantive one,
and there the evidence is strong and one-directional: fusion lost in every cell.

## 8. The four traps — where the marks are

Each of these silently invalidates a SHAP-versus-LIME comparison, and **none of them is
visible in the output when it goes wrong**. If the advisor asks what separates this from a
class project, this is the answer.

### Trap 1 — the two methods must explain the *same quantity*

LIME explains predicted **probability**. SHAP's TreeExplainer on boosted models returns
**log-odds margins by default**. Compare them naively and you are contrasting two different
quantities on two different scales — and the numbers will still look perfectly reasonable.

**What we did:** required probability-space attributions everywhere, and then *verified* it
rather than trusting the flag — we check that `base value + sum of attributions` reproduces
the model's `predict_proba` output. Median additivity error across cells is **3.5 × 10⁻⁸**.
One cell out of 18 failed the pre-registered gate; see Part 10.

### Trap 2 — the reference distribution must be real data

It is common to summarise SHAP's background set with k-means. That produces **synthetic
points**, because averaging one-hot columns gives fractional values the model has never
seen. Interventional TreeSHAP evaluated against such off-manifold references violates its
own additivity guarantee.

**We measured the damage:** additivity error of **9.9 × 10⁻³** with k-means, against
**7 × 10⁻⁹** with real training rows, on the Heart/LightGBM cell. So the background is a
seeded, class-stratified subsample of **actual training rows** — which also keeps tree SHAP
deterministic.

### Trap 3 — one-hot encoding fragments a feature across several model inputs

One original feature can become several model columns. Left unaggregated, rankings from
different models would be over different vocabularies, and "delete the top five features"
would delete five **columns** — under-perturbing a 13-feature dataset spread over 25 columns
while leaving an 8-feature dataset untouched. The comparison would be quietly unfair between
datasets.

**What we did:** attributions are summed over each feature's columns before any ranking, and
perturbation operates on whole feature groups. The column-to-feature map is emitted with the
processed data and unit-tested.

### Trap 4 — a fidelity score has no scale without a control

Deleting *any* few of eight features moves a prediction. So an AOPC of 0.31 on its own is
uninterpretable — you cannot tell whether it reflects a good explanation or just the fact
that removing information hurts.

**What we did:** every fidelity number is reported against a uniformly random ranking, which
scores **0.075**. That is what makes SHAP's 0.260 and LIME's 0.224 meaningful.

## 9. Pre-registration and reproducibility

### 9.1 What pre-registration means here

Every research question, hypothesis, metric formula, statistical test, significance level and
correction procedure was written into `docs/PROTOCOL.md` and **committed to git in its own
commit, before any commit containing results**. The repository history is therefore itself
evidence that the analysis was not adjusted after seeing the outcomes. Any change after the
freeze is recorded in an amendment log with a date and a reason — never by a silent edit.

**Why it matters for this thesis specifically:** a negative result on RQ6 is only credible if
we could not have moved the goalposts. We could not — the strict bar and the clause saying
"a null result on contribution 3 is a valid and reportable outcome" were both in the protocol
before anything ran.

### 9.2 It paid for itself three times

Three real errors were caught by tests that existed only because the protocol demanded them,
and **none of the three would have been visible in the results**:

1. The **additivity check** caught a background-construction error that violated SHAP's own
   additivity by about one per cent on LightGBM cells, while every other cell looked healthy.
2. A test requiring **every confidence interval to contain its own point estimate** caught a
   BCa bootstrap being misapplied to a non-smooth statistic.
3. A test on the **RBO floor** caught that raw RBO is not comparable across datasets of
   different width.

All three are recorded in the protocol's amendment log.

### 9.3 What "reproducible" actually means in this repository

- dependencies pinned to exact versions (`requirements.lock.txt`)
- source datasets vendored with SHA-256 checksums **verified on every run** — a mismatch is
  a hard failure, not a warning
- a manifest recording a digest for every artefact, plus the git commit, the seed set, the
  resolved library versions and the hardware
- **every number, figure and table generated by code; none transcribed by hand**
- global seed 42; repeat *r* uses seed 1000 + *r*; bootstrap resample *b* uses seed 2000 + *b*
- `make all` reproduces the whole study from raw data to typeset PDFs

## 10. Limitations — state these before anyone asks

- **Three datasets, all small, all clinical.** The per-cell tests have real statistical
  power, but generalisation *across datasets* rests on three points. The difficulty gradient
  is a design compensation, not a substitute for breadth.
- **Perturbation baselines are off-manifold.** Replacing a feature with the training median
  creates rows the data-generating process would not produce. A marginal-draw variant did not
  reverse the ordering, but both share an independence assumption.
- **The plausibility reference sets are not ground truth.** Compiled from cited literature and
  frozen before any result, they are still incomplete, non-causal, and shaped by what their
  sources chose to measure. That is why RQ5 is corroborating evidence only.
- **Retraining stability covers three of six models** — restricted in advance on compute
  grounds, not after seeing results.
- **Timing is single-machine.** The 24.8× gap reflects a structural difference, but its exact
  magnitude is hardware-specific.
- **No human evaluation.** Every metric here is a property of the model–explanation pair. We
  do not measure whether a clinician finds an explanation useful.
- **A faithful post-hoc explanation of a black box is still not an interpretable model**
  (Rudin's argument, which we cite rather than dodge).

### 10.1 The additivity gate failure — volunteer this one

In the PIMA/LightGBM cell, **5 of 150** explained instances reconstruct `predict_proba` to
worse than the 5 × 10⁻³ tolerance (worst case 0.094), against a cell median of 1.1 × 10⁻⁹.
So the pre-registered gate is **not satisfied** for that cell. The discrepancy is insensitive
to background size, and we did not attribute it; the obvious candidate — coincidence with a
median-imputed value — was tested and rejected.

Our two pre-registered options were to force that cell into log-odds space, which would
remove it from every cross-explainer comparison, or to widen the gate. **We did neither.**
Widening a gate after seeing the result it fails is precisely what pre-registration exists to
prevent. The cell is retained, the failure is stated in the paper, and every hypothesis tally
is additionally reported with the cell excluded — no decision in the hypothesis table changes.

Volunteering this is a strength, not a weakness: it is the protocol visibly working.

## 12. Advisor questions, with answers

Short answers you can actually say out loud. Do not read them; know them.

### About what the work is

**"What did you actually do?"**
> We compared SHAP and LIME under one controlled protocol — six classifiers, three medical
> datasets, eighteen combinations, 1,746 explained predictions — on agreement, three kinds of
> stability, faithfulness, cost and plausibility. And we proposed a method to combine them,
> fidelity-weighted rank fusion, tested against two published fusion baselines. The
> comparison gave clear answers; the combination did not clear its bar, and we explain why.

**"What is new here? What is yours?"**
> Three things. The benchmark design — varying model family, dataset and explainer together
> under one leakage-safe protocol with pre-registered hypotheses. The stability
> decomposition into explainer randomness, input perturbation and retraining variance. And
> the fidelity-weighted fusion rule with its leakage-safe calibration split. The metrics are
> all from published literature and cited; the idea of aggregating explanations is prior work
> and we cite that too.

**"Did you invent explanation fusion?"**
> No, and we say so in the paper. Mean-rank fusion and reciprocal-rank fusion are published;
> we use them as baselines. What is ours is weighting the two explainers by their *measured*
> fidelity instead of equally, fitting that weight on a disjoint calibration split so the
> rule cannot be tuned on its own test set, and testing against the better of the two rather
> than their mean.

### About the hybrid and the title

**"Your method is in the title but it did not work. Why is that a contribution?"**
> Because we set the bar honestly and reported what we found. The bar was to beat the
> *better* of SHAP and LIME on the same cell — not their average, which anything would beat.
> The null outcome was registered as a valid result before we ran anything, so we could not
> quietly redefine success. And we can explain the mechanism: fusion needs complementary
> inputs, and these two are not complementary — they agree substantially and one is
> consistently better, so averaging can only dilute the stronger signal. A paper that got a
> positive result by luck and could not explain it would be worth less than this.

**"Then why put it in the title at all?"**
> Because it is one of the three contributions and a reader deserves to know the paper
> proposes a method as well as running a comparison. "And Its Limits" is the honest half of
> that claim — we bounded when explanation fusion is worth attempting, which is a result.

**"What would make fusion work?"**
> Base explainers that are genuinely complementary — for example a gradient-based method and
> a perturbation-based one, or explainers of comparable quality rather than one dominating.
> That is the natural follow-up, and it is written into the future work.

### About the findings

**"ρ = 0.74 — is that good agreement or bad?"**
> Substantial, and higher than the disagreement literature would suggest. But the top-5 lists
> still differ by a feature at the median, so it is not high enough to treat the two as
> interchangeable. Both halves of that sentence are the finding.

**"So can I just use the features both methods agree on?"**
> That is exactly what our result warns against. Taking the intersection silently drops about
> a feature from each list and gives you no way to know which. It looks like a robustness
> check but it is not one.

**"Should we always use SHAP then?"**
> Wherever a tree backend exists, yes on this evidence — it is more faithful, about 25 times
> cheaper, and much more robust to small input changes. LIME's remaining case is that it works
> on any model at all, which our stacking cells show is a real advantage.

**"SHAP is deterministic. Why measure its stability?"**
> Because determinism and robustness are different properties, and that is one of our three
> contributions. Tree SHAP returns bit-identical numbers on re-runs — we declared that in
> advance as a property of the method. But its cross-refit agreement is 0.83: refit the same
> model on a resample of the same data and the global ranking moves. The instability lives in
> the model, not the explainer, and determinism cannot remove it.

**"Why is agreement lowest on the easiest dataset? Is that significant?"**
> It is not statistically established — H1c was not rejected, and with three datasets there is
> no power to test a trend. As an observation, agreement was 0.63 on the easiest task against
> 0.76 and 0.78 on the harder two. Our proposed explanation is feature redundancy rather than
> difficulty: when several features are individually sufficient, two explainers can both be
> locally faithful while naming different ones.

**"What is AOPC, in one sentence?"**
> Delete the features an explanation ranks first, replace them with the training median, and
> measure how far the model's own predicted probability falls; average that over the first ten
> deletions (or all of them, if the dataset has fewer than ten features). A faithful ranking
> makes it fall fast.

**"Why do you need a random-ranking control?"**
> Because deleting any few features moves a prediction, so an AOPC value alone has no scale.
> The control scores 0.075; SHAP scores 0.260. Without the control the second number means
> nothing.

### About the method and rigour

**"How do I know you did not adjust anything after seeing the results?"**
> The protocol with all eleven hypotheses, metric formulas, tests and thresholds was committed
> to git in its own commit before any commit containing a result. The history is the evidence.
> Every later change is in an amendment log with a date and a reason. And one gate failed —
> PIMA/LightGBM's additivity check — which we report rather than widen, because widening a
> gate after seeing it fail is what pre-registration exists to prevent.

**"Why only three datasets?"**
> Honest answer: compute and scope for an undergraduate thesis. We chose three that span a
> difficulty gradient rather than three similar ones, so the design buys some of what breadth
> would have bought. It is listed first in our limitations — generalisation across datasets
> rests on three points.

**"Why medical data if you make no medical claim?"**
> Because they are the standard small public tabular benchmarks and the setting where
> explanation matters most in practice. The diseases are benchmarks for a computer-science
> question; no model here is fit for diagnostic use and we say that in the ethics statement.

**"Why no deep learning?"**
> Tree ensembles are what actually performs best on tabular data, and they are the models that
> need post-hoc explanation. Adding a neural network would add an axis without answering the
> question we asked.

**"Why no SMOTE / oversampling?"**
> The imbalance is mild, and synthetic oversampling changes the data manifold that LIME
> samples from and SHAP draws its background from. It would confound the exact comparison
> under study. The decision is recorded in the protocol so it reads as a choice, not an
> oversight.

**"Where do the numbers in the paper come from?"**
> All of them from code. The pipeline writes `results/`, a script turns those into the LaTeX
> macros in `tables/findings.tex`, and the paper uses the macros — so no number is typed by
> hand. `make all` regenerates the study from raw data to PDF.

**"What separates this from a class project?"**
> Four methodological traps that silently invalidate comparisons of this kind, which we found,
> closed and documented: the two explainers must explain the same quantity and we verify it;
> the SHAP background must be real rows, not k-means centroids; one-hot columns must be
> aggregated back to features before ranking; and fidelity has no scale without a random
> control. None of the four is visible in the output when it goes wrong.

## 15. Glossary

- **AOPC** — Area Over the Perturbation Curve. The average fall in the model's predicted
  probability as you delete the top-1, top-2, … top-10 ranked features. Higher = more faithful.
- **Additivity check** — verifying that base value + sum of attributions equals the model's
  actual predicted probability. Our guard against comparing different output spaces.
- **BCa bootstrap** — a bias-corrected bootstrap confidence interval. Ours was misapplied to a
  non-smooth statistic once; a protocol test caught it.
- **Bootstrap resample** — a sample drawn with replacement from the training data, used to
  refit the model and see whether the explanation survives.
- **Calibration split** — the 30 % of explained instances used to fit the fusion weight,
  disjoint from the 70 % on which RQ6 is tested.
- **Cell** — one (dataset, model) pair. There are 18.
- **Coefficient of variation (CV)** — standard deviation divided by mean; how much an
  explainer's numbers wobble across re-runs.
- **Comprehensiveness / sufficiency** — ERASER-style fidelity measures reported alongside AOPC.
- **Effect size (rank-biserial)** — how big a difference is, independent of sample size. With
  hundreds of instances per cell, this matters more than the p-value.
- **Fidelity** — how well an explanation reflects what the model actually does. Measured, not
  assumed.
- **Holm correction** — an adjustment for testing many hypotheses at once, so that running
  eighteen tests does not manufacture a false positive.
- **Jaccard@k** — overlap of two top-k sets: size of intersection divided by size of union.
- **Leakage** — when information from the test data influences training or tuning, making
  results look better than they are. Our two-track design exists to prevent one specific kind.
- **LIME** — Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations: fits a simple linear model in the
  neighbourhood of one prediction.
- **Local Lipschitz estimate** — the largest change in explanation per unit change in input,
  over a small neighbourhood. Lower = more robust.
- **Log-odds vs probability space** — two different scales a model's output can be attributed
  in. Mixing them silently invalidates a comparison (trap 1).
- **Nested cross-validation** — an outer loop for honest performance estimates, an inner loop
  for hyperparameter selection. Prevents tuning on the data you score on.
- **Off-manifold** — an input that the real data-generating process would never produce, such
  as a row created by replacing one feature with a median value.
- **Pre-registration** — writing hypotheses and analysis choices down, and committing them,
  before seeing any result.
- **RBO (rank-biased overlap)** — a top-weighted similarity between two ranked lists that does
  not require choosing a cut-off. Has a floor that depends on the number of features.
- **RRF (reciprocal-rank fusion)** — a published fusion baseline: each list contributes
  1/(60 + rank).
- **SHAP** — SHapley Additive exPlanations: attributes a prediction to features using the
  Shapley value from cooperative game theory.
- **Spearman ρ / Kendall τ** — correlation between two rankings; 1 means identical order.
- **Stacking ensemble** — a model that combines several base models through a meta-learner. In
  this study it is the one model with no tree-specific SHAP backend.
- **TreeSHAP / TreeExplainer** — the exact, fast SHAP algorithm for tree models. Deterministic.
- **Wilcoxon signed-rank test** — a non-parametric paired test; our default throughout.
