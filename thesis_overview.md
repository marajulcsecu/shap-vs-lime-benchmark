# Thesis Overview — SHAP or LIME? A Controlled Comparison

**Course:** ENG 672 Technical Communication and Writing · **Revised:** 2026-08-29 · **v2**

> The original version of this document is preserved at `docs/thesis_overview_ORIGINAL.md`.
> §7 lists exactly what changed and why, so the revision is auditable.

---

## 1. In 30 seconds

We compare the two most widely used post-hoc explanation methods — **SHAP** and **LIME** — across
**6 classifiers × 3 public clinical tabular datasets = 18 model–dataset cells**, measuring five things:
do they **agree**, are they **stable**, are they **faithful** to the model, what do they **cost**, and
are their top features **plausible** against cited prior literature. We then test whether **fusing**
their rankings beats the better of the two alone. Every number in the paper is generated by code from
a frozen, pre-registered protocol, and the whole study reproduces with one command.

**Title:**

> SHAP versus LIME on Clinical Tabular Data: A Reproducible Benchmark of Explanation Agreement,
> Stability, Fidelity, and Rank-Level Consensus

---

## 2. Why this is worth doing

High-accuracy models are already deployed where decisions affect people, and regulation increasingly
requires those decisions to be explainable. SHAP and LIME are the default tools for that job. They
rest on different theory — Shapley values from cooperative game theory versus a locally fitted linear
surrogate — so they can disagree about the same prediction, and a practitioner has little guidance on
which to trust when they do.

Existing comparisons tend to hold too much fixed: one model, or one dataset, or metrics defined
differently from paper to paper, usually without statistical testing or released code. The gap is not
a missing algorithm; it is a **missing controlled comparison**. That is a gap an undergraduate thesis
can actually close, which is why we are closing that one rather than inventing a method.

---

## 3. What we claim — stated precisely

1. **A reproducible benchmark.** 2 explainers + 3 fusion rules + 1 control, across 18 cells, under one
   leakage-safe protocol, with 11 pre-registered hypotheses, non-parametric tests, Holm correction,
   effect sizes, and bootstrap confidence intervals. Every reported value regenerated from code.
2. **A decomposition of explanation stability into three separable sources** — explainer randomness,
   input perturbation, and model retraining variance — measured on the same models. This distinguishes
   *reproducible* from *robust*, which single-notion stability studies conflate.
3. **A controlled evaluation of three explanation-fusion rules** (mean-rank, reciprocal-rank fusion,
   and a fidelity-weighted variant) against a strict bar: beat the *better* of SHAP and LIME, not their
   average.

**What we do not claim.** We do not claim to have invented explanation aggregation — that idea exists
and we cite it. Our contribution there is the evaluation, not the invention. We make no medical claims;
the three diseases are benchmarks, not a clinical study. And we are not targeting a journal that
requires novelty — the paper is *prepared in IEEE conference format* because the assignment asked for a
conference paper.

A null result on claim 3 is fine and is pre-registered as such. If no fusion rule wins, the finding is
that high base agreement leaves no complementary signal to exploit — which is itself worth reporting
and links directly back to RQ1.

## 4. The six research questions

| # | Question | Primary metric |
|---|----------|----------------|
| RQ1 | Do SHAP and LIME rank features concordantly, and does that change with model family and task difficulty? | Spearman ρ, Kendall τ, Jaccard@k, RBO |
| RQ2 | How stable are explanations — under explainer randomness (2a), input perturbation (2b), and model retraining (2c)? | CV; local Lipschitz estimate; cross-resample agreement |
| RQ3 | Are the explanations faithful to the model, compared against a **random-ranking control**? | AOPC deletion, AUC-insertion, comprehensiveness, sufficiency |
| RQ4 | What does each explanation cost, and what fidelity does that buy? | median s/explanation; fidelity-per-second |
| RQ5 | Do top features overlap a pre-registered, cited reference set? *(weakest metric — corroborating only)* | Jaccard@5, Precision@5 |
| RQ6 | Does rank fusion beat the better of SHAP and LIME? | ΔAOPC vs. max(SHAP, LIME) |

Full formulas, all 11 hypotheses with named tests and α, and the pre-registered fallbacks are in
**`docs/PROTOCOL.md`**, frozen and committed before any result exists.

| Dimension | Decision |
|-----------|----------|
| **Datasets** | PIMA Diabetes (768×8, hard), Heart Disease/Cleveland (303×13, medium), Chronic Kidney Disease (400×24, easy) — spanning a deliberate **difficulty gradient** |

| **Protocol** | Two tracks: nested 5×3 CV for performance; a single fixed 80/20 split for explanations |
| **Statistics** | Non-parametric throughout, Holm-corrected within family, effect sizes and 95 % BCa CIs on everything |
| **Framing** | Computer-science methodology study; no medical claims |
| **Compute** | ≈ 2 hours full run on 12 cores / 8 GB RAM; ≈ 5 minutes in QUICK mode |

## 7. What changed from v1, and why

Five revisions. Each strengthens a claim rather than shrinking the work.

**7.1 The "novel hybrid consensus method" became a controlled evaluation of three fusion rules.**
Rank-averaging two explainers is a small idea, and explanation aggregation already exists in the
literature. Calling it *the* novel contribution invites the one question the work cannot answer — how
does it differ from prior fusion work? An evaluation claim cannot be refuted by a citation, so the
reframing is strictly safer, and adding two more fusion rules (reciprocal-rank fusion and a
fidelity-weighted variant) makes the evaluation more informative than testing one rule would have been.

**7.2 "Publishable IEEE Access paper" became "prepared in IEEE conference format."** IEEE Access review
turns on genuine novelty. Writing that we target it hands the advisor a bar the work is not trying to
clear. The paper still looks and reads like a conference submission.

**7.3 RQ2 (stability) was rebuilt, because as written it was nearly vacuous.** SHAP's TreeExplainer is
*deterministic* — re-running it returns identical attributions — so "coefficient of variation across
re-runs" is exactly zero for four of the six models before any experiment runs. Reporting that as a
discovery would be an error. Stability is now split into three separable sources, only one of which is
degenerate for tree SHAP, and that degeneracy is declared in advance in the protocol. The new
component — does the ranking survive **retraining** on a slightly different sample? — is the
interesting one, and it separates a fragile *model* from a fragile *explainer*.

**7.4 A random-ranking control was added to every fidelity measurement.** Deletion-based AOPC has no
absolute scale: removing any 5 of 8 features moves a prediction. Without a random baseline, "SHAP
achieved AOPC 0.31" cannot be interpreted. This addition is cheap and it is the difference between a
number and a result.

**7.5 SHAP and LIME are now forced to explain the same quantity.** LIME explains predicted
*probability*; SHAP's tree backend defaults to *log-odds margin*. Comparing them directly compares
different quantities on different scales — a common and invalidating error in this literature. SHAP
therefore runs with `model_output="probability"`, with a pre-registered fallback if it proves too slow.

Also: RQ5 was kept but demoted. Its reference feature sets are frozen and cited before results exist,
it is labelled the weakest metric in the study, and it is reported as corroborating evidence only —
because a literature-derived feature list is not ground truth, and treating it as one would be the
easiest thing in this study to attack.
