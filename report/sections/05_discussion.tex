\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What a practitioner should take from this}
The agreement number cuts both ways, and it is worth being precise about it. At median
$\rho = \MedianRho{}$ with top-5 overlap of \MedianJaccardFive{}, the two methods are
not the wildly divergent pair that some of the disagreement literature implies: they
clear a moderate-association bar in \HOneRejected{} of \HOneTotal{} cells. But one
feature in each top-five list still differs, so they are not interchangeable either. The
practical consequence is that reading the intersection of the two is not a robustness
check --- it silently drops a feature from each explanation --- and if a decision turns
on which features appear in a top-five list, the choice of explainer is part of that
decision and belongs in the record.

On the evidence here the default should be \shap{} where a tree backend exists. It was
the more faithful of the two on \aopc{} and insertion AUC, roughly \SpeedFactor{} times
cheaper, deterministic given a fixed background, and the more robust to input
perturbation. That is not a theoretical preference; on these eighteen cells it wins on
every axis we measured except model-agnosticism. \lime{}'s case is precisely
model-agnosticism --- and our stacking cells show what that is worth, since they admit
no tree explainer at all.

\subsection{Reproducible is not robust, and the difference has teeth}
The stability decomposition is the result we would most want carried forward. Tree
\shap{}'s re-run variance is exactly zero, and a study that measured stability only
that way would report it as perfectly stable. Its cross-refit agreement was
\RetrainShap{}, meaning the global ranking moves when the model is refitted on a
bootstrap resample of the same training data. The instability is in the model, not the
explainer, and no amount of determinism in the attribution method removes it. Anyone
reporting explanation stability should say which of the three things they measured.

\subsection{Why fusion did not help}
The null result on \RQ{6} is intelligible rather than disappointing, and it follows
from \RQ{1}. Fusion pays off when the inputs are individually noisy but their errors
are uncorrelated --- the ensemble argument. Here neither condition holds. The two agree
substantially ($\rho = \MedianRho{}$), so there is little independent signal for fusion
to recover, and one of them is consistently better: with \shap{} ahead on fidelity in
most cells, averaging its ranks with a weaker and largely redundant signal moves the
result toward the weaker one. The effect is consistent rather than marginal --- fusion
lost in every one of the \HSixTotal{} cells, with a median rank-biserial effect of
$-0.80$. The fidelity-weighted rule was designed to counter exactly that
by learning $w$ from a held-out calibration split, and it was usually the best of the
three, but a weight fitted per cell on a small sample cannot recover the gap. We read
the outcome as evidence that explanation aggregation needs \emph{complementary} base
explainers, and that \shap{} and \lime{} on tree models are not complementary enough.

\subsection{On pre-registration in a study of this size}
Freezing the protocol before running cost little and repaid it three times. The
additivity check we wrote to enforce probability-space comparability caught a
background-construction error that violated \shap{}'s own additivity by one per cent
on LightGBM cells while every other cell looked healthy. A test asserting that every
reported interval contains its own point estimate caught a misapplication of BCa
bootstrap to a non-smooth statistic. A test on the rank-biased-overlap floor caught
that raw \rbo{} is not comparable across datasets of different width. None of these
would have been visible in the results; all three are recorded in the protocol's
amendment log with dates and reasons. We mention this because the alternative --- a
comparison study whose analysis choices are made after seeing the numbers --- is the
normal case in this literature, and it is cheap to avoid.

\section{Practical guidance}
Compressing the results into advice a practitioner can act on:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{If the model is a tree ensemble, use SHAP.} It was more faithful,
    roughly \SpeedFactor{}$\times$ cheaper, deterministic given a fixed background, and
    more robust to input perturbation. LIME's remaining advantage is that it works when
    no tree backend exists.
  \item \textbf{Fix and record the background distribution.} SHAP's attributions depend
    on it, and constructing it from synthetic summary points rather than real rows can
    break additivity outright (Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls}).
  \item \textbf{Do not read agreement between the two as validation.} At median
    $\rho = \MedianRho{}$, taking the intersection of two top-five lists discards about
    half of each.
  \item \textbf{Report which stability you measured.} Re-run determinism, perturbation
    robustness, and retraining stability are different properties with different
    remedies, and the first is the least informative.
  \item \textbf{Always include a random-ranking control} when reporting any
    perturbation-based fidelity number.
\end{enumerate}
