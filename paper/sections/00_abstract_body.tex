SHAP and LIME are the two tools most often used to explain what a model has learned from a
table of data. Both take one prediction and rank the features by how much each one
mattered. They work in different ways and often disagree about the same prediction, which
leaves anyone who runs both with two answers and no way to choose. Earlier comparisons do
not settle the question: they change one thing at a time, and none checks its scores
against a random list of features. This paper compares the two on four things a user would
care about. Do the two agree, does the same answer come back, does the answer match what
the model really uses, and how long does each one take? The rules were fixed first, and
the study is wide: six classifiers, three public clinical datasets, eighteen
model--dataset pairs, and eleven predictions written into version control before any
result existed. We also split explanation stability, usually reported as one number, into
three separate things: chance inside the tool, small changes to the input, and training
the model again. Alongside the comparison we propose a hybrid method, and test it fairly.
Rather than trust the two tools equally, we trust each one as much as it earns. An
explanation is faithful when deleting the features it puts on top makes the prediction
fall fastest. We measure that on a slice of data that is never used for testing, and it
sets the weight. We compare our rule with two published ways of merging explanations, and
set a strict test: a merged ranking must beat the better of the two tools, not their
average. The two tools largely agree but are not interchangeable. In the typical pair they
share four of their five most important features and disagree on the fifth, and they agree
least on the \emph{easiest} dataset rather than the hardest. SHAP is the more faithful, is
far steadier when the input is nudged, and is about \SpeedFactor{} times faster whenever
the model is a tree. Repeating exactly is not the same as being stable, because SHAP's
ranking still moves when the model is trained again on a slightly different sample.
Merging the two, our own rule included, never beat the better single tool. This is a real
finding, not a failed experiment: merging pays off when the inputs cover each other's
mistakes, and these two do not. Every number here is produced by code, and the study
re-runs with one command.
