Post-hoc explanation methods are the standard tool for interpreting high-accuracy
tabular classifiers, and SHAP and LIME dominate practice. They rest on different
theory --- cooperative-game attribution versus a locally fitted linear surrogate ---
so they can disagree about the same prediction, yet practitioners have little
guidance on which to trust when they do. Existing comparisons hold too much fixed:
typically one model, or one dataset, with metrics defined differently from paper to
paper and no statistical testing. We contribute a reproducible benchmark that varies
all three axes at once: two explainers, three rank-fusion rules, and a random-ranking
control, across six classifiers and three public clinical datasets --- eighteen
model--dataset cells --- under a single leakage-safe protocol with eleven
pre-registered hypotheses, non-parametric tests, Holm correction, effect sizes, and
bootstrap intervals. We additionally decompose explanation stability into three
separable sources that single-notion studies conflate: explainer randomness, input
perturbation, and model retraining variance. Across \NumCells{} cells and \NumExplained{} explained instances the two methods agree moderately but not interchangeably --- median Spearman $\rho$ of \MedianRho{} and top-5 overlap of \MedianJaccardFive{}, clearing a pre-registered bar of $0.50$ in \HOneRejected{} of \HOneTotal{} cells while still disagreeing on about a third of each top-five list. SHAP is the more faithful (\aopc{} \AopcShap{} versus \AopcLime{}; both far above the random control at \AopcRandom{}), roughly \SpeedFactor{}$\times$ cheaper, and markedly more robust to input perturbation (\LipschitzShap{} versus \LipschitzLime{}). Rank fusion of the two improved on the better of them in \HSixRejected{} of \HSixTotal{} cells --- a null result we pre-registered as valid, and one that follows from the level of agreement: fusion needs complementary inputs, and these are not.
