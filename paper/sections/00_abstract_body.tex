SHAP and LIME dominate post-hoc explanation of tabular classifiers, rest on different
theory, and routinely disagree about the same prediction, yet practitioners have little
controlled evidence on which to trust. Existing comparisons vary one axis at a time,
define fidelity differently from paper to paper, omit a random-ranking control, and do not
state which output space their attributions occupy. We propose a hybrid consensus method
--- \emph{fidelity-weighted rank fusion}, which combines the two explainers in proportion
to the faithfulness each demonstrates on a disjoint calibration split --- and evaluate it,
together with two published fusion baselines, inside a reproducible benchmark that varies
model family, dataset, and explainer at once: six classifiers across three public clinical
datasets, eighteen model--dataset cells, under one leakage-safe protocol with eleven
hypotheses pre-registered in version control before any result existed. We additionally
decompose explanation stability into three sources that single-notion studies conflate.
The two explainers agree substantially but are not interchangeable: at the median they
share four of their five top-ranked features and disagree on the fifth, and agreement is
lowest on the \emph{easiest} task rather than the hardest. SHAP is the more faithful, markedly more robust to input
perturbation, and roughly \SpeedFactor{}$\times$ cheaper wherever a tree backend exists,
while a bit-identical explainer proves not to be a stable one: its global ranking still
shifts when the model is refitted on a resample of the same data. Rank fusion, including
our fidelity-weighted rule, failed to beat the better of the two explainers in any cell
--- a negative result, pre-registered as valid, that follows from the level of agreement:
fusion needs complementary inputs and these are not complementary. Every number here is
generated by code; the study reproduces with one command.
