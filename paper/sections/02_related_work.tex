\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{The two methods}
\shap{} assigns each feature the Shapley value of its marginal contribution to a
prediction, giving local accuracy and consistency by
construction~\cite{lundberg2017unified}. \textsc{TreeSHAP} makes this tractable for
tree ensembles in polynomial time~\cite{lundberg2020local}. \lime{} instead perturbs
the instance, weights the perturbations by proximity, and fits a sparse linear
surrogate whose coefficients are the explanation~\cite{ribeiro2016why}. The
difference is not cosmetic: \shap{} is an attribution of the model's output, while
\lime{} is a local approximation of the model's function, and the two answer
different questions about the same prediction.

\subsection{Evaluating an explanation}
Because there is no ground-truth attribution, evaluation is indirect. Perturbation
tests remove features in explanation order and measure how fast the prediction
degrades, summarised as area over the perturbation curve~\cite{samek2017evaluating};
the \textsc{eraser} benchmark formalised the complementary notions of
comprehensiveness and sufficiency~\cite{deyoung2020eraser}. Hooker et
al.~\cite{hooker2019benchmark} showed that such tests are confounded by the
off-manifold inputs that perturbation creates, a caveat we address directly in
Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls}. Robustness --- similar inputs should receive similar
explanations --- is usually estimated as a local Lipschitz
constant~\cite{alvarezmelis2018robustness}. For comparing ranked lists, rank-biased
overlap weights the head of the list without requiring a cut-off
$k$~\cite{webber2010similarity}.

\subsection{Known failure modes}
\shap{}'s guarantees are weaker in practice than in theory: the choice of background
distribution changes the attributions~\cite{sundararajan2020many}, and Shapley values
answer a question that may not be the one asked~\cite{kumar2020problems}. Both methods
can be deliberately fooled by an adversarial
model~\cite{slack2020fooling}. Rudin~\cite{rudin2019stop} argues that post-hoc
explanation of a black box is the wrong goal for high-stakes decisions. We take these
as reasons for careful measurement rather than as settled verdicts.

\subsection{Aggregating explanations}
Combining several explainers has been proposed before, both as an ensemble of
attributions~\cite{rieger2020simple} and as a framework for aggregating explanation
outputs~\cite{bhatt2020aggregating}; rank fusion itself is standard in information
retrieval, where reciprocal-rank fusion is a strong and very simple
baseline~\cite{cormack2009reciprocal}. We therefore make no novelty claim for fusion.
What is missing is an evaluation of such rules under controlled conditions against a
bar that a practitioner would care about, which is what Section~\ref{sec:rq6} supplies.
